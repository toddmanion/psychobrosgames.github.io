The thirty-second version

Read the game. Just read it.

Finally, a game where the voices in our head are also credited as skills.

Disco Elysium is our reigning best-writing-in-a-video-game champion. It's a proper detective story, a proper political novel, and a proper character study, and the mechanics are all about talking, thinking and rolling dice with parts of your own personality. Nothing else feels like it. Nothing has really tried.

Okay, what is this thing?

You wake up on the floor of a hostel room you have destroyed. You have no memory. There is a corpse in a tree outside. Twenty-four different parts of your brain start talking to you — Empathy, Volition, Inland Empire, Electrochemistry — and immediately begin arguing about whether the tie strangling the ceiling fan is your friend. It absolutely is, by the way. It's a great tie.

The systems are all dialogue and dice. Every skill is a voice; every voice can pipe up with a check; every check succeeds or fails and changes the case. You can be a communist detective, a coked-up genius, a sad philosopher, or a legally responsible adult. The murder is real, the politics are pointed, and the writing is genuinely as good as any novel any of us read this year.

Why it escaped the group chat

  • There is essentially no combat — checks are made through skills, dice and dialogue trees.
  • Twenty-four skills, each of which is a voice in your protagonist's head.
  • The Final Cut adds full voice acting to a game that was already very talky.

Three dads enter

01

Dad Who Skipped the Tutorial

The one time I read every line of dialogue in a game.

I don't normally read tooltips. I read every skill check, every internal monologue, every book my character narrated aloud to himself. I took eight in-game days to leave the hostel and considered it well spent. I also failed the necktie check on purpose because I liked how the tie kept talking. I was correct.

02

The Descent Guy

The skill system is a personality generator.

I built a fully specced Logic and Encyclopedia detective, hit every white check by savescumming, and then did a second run as a purely physical man who solved the case with his heart. I've got strong opinions about which skills are traps. I want you to know Shivers is not a trap. Shivers is, in my own words, 'the whole game once you get it.'

03

Normal-ish Dad

The rare no-combat game we all finished.

It's roughly thirty hours long, it has essentially no combat, and I could play it in one-hour sessions without losing the thread because the game keeps track of who you're talking to and what they said last. I finished it. So did the other two, which is basically unheard of for anything with dialogue trees. That's the review.

Who it is for and what to watch

Invite these people

  • Anyone who used to read
  • People whose favourite bit of Fallout was talking to a mushroom
  • Dads who want a serious game they can play in short sittings

Dad caveats

  • There is a lot of reading, and we mean a lot
  • The politics are not a garnish; the game has actual opinions
  • You will fail an important check and it will actually make the story better

Receipts

All three of us finished it on PC over the winter of that year; two of us went back for a second run to see the different political vision quests. Details checked against the game's official Steam listing.

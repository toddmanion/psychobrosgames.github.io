The thirty-second version

Roadside Americana with teeth.

Road trip rule one: never trust the dinosaur-shaped tourist trap.

We haven't played it, but Terrible Lizards starts from a location horror games have barely touched: a sun-faded tourist trap built around fake dinosaurs. The missing-child mystery gives us a reason to explore, and 1992 is a smart date because it takes away the exits modern technology usually creates. This is our most 'oh no, and yes' pitch of the week.

Okay, what is this thing?

The car dies outside Dalton's Dinos, a deteriorating Route 66 attraction in New Mexico. It's 1992, a child is missing, and the creatures stalking the property are more dangerous than the fibreglass statues around them suggest. That's already three of our favourite horror ingredients — bad decade, worse place, real reason to walk further in — and we haven't even opened the game.

The scale is the smartest part of the pitch. It's a contained attraction, an investigation and a chase, not another sprawling apocalypse. That means the location itself is the game's strongest asset, and the trailer is confident enough in the setting that it doesn't try to sell you on the world beyond the parking lot. Which is where every good horror movie stays.

Why it escaped the group chat

  • The story is set in 1992: no phones, no rideshares, no easy escape.
  • Dalton's Dinos is a deserted Route 66 dinosaur attraction in New Mexico.
  • You're investigating a missing-child case while creatures actively hunt you.

Three dads enter

01

Dad Who Skipped the Tutorial

A clear mystery pulls me in where pure survival can't.

I don't play survival horror because the loop punishes me just for existing. A specific mystery gives every scary corridor a purpose, and that's exactly the reason I keep going. Whether I stay depends on generous checkpoints and chases that tell me where safety actually is, because I will absolutely stop if the game punishes me for learning.

02

The Descent Guy

The attraction needs mechanics worth learning.

I only respect a memorable monster when its behaviour can be studied and used against it. If every encounter is a scripted sprint, the location carries more weight than the play, which is fine for one playthrough and not a game I replay. I want the fibreglass dinosaurs to have opinions.

03

Normal-ish Dad

The setting sells a focused, seasonal horror night.

A contained roadside attraction is easy to pitch to the other two and different enough that I'll remember it afterwards. I've already got it earmarked for a specific late-October evening once the kids are down. The ideal version stays tight instead of stretching the premise until the dinosaurs stop being scary and start being scenery.

Who it is for and what to watch

Invite these people

  • Horror players who value setting and mystery over gore
  • Fans of analog-era stories where you can't call for help
  • Groups who want one focused seasonal playthrough

Dad caveats

  • Checkpoint generosity when the chases start
  • Enemy behaviour depth: is there more than sprint or die
  • Whether the missing-child story earns its darker material

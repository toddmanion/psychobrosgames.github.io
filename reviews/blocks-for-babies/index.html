The thirty-second version

Two genres slam into each other on purpose.

Tetris got a shotgun. We have follow-up questions.

We haven't played it, but Blocks for Babies has a pitch that survives the elevator ride: stack the board, jump into it, shoot the mess you made. The deciding factor is whether upgrades turn that contrast into strategy instead of asking players to alternate between two disconnected minigames. If they do, this is the wildest party demo of the year.

Okay, what is this thing?

A run switches between arcade block-stacking and first-person shooting, which is one of those sentences we had to read twice. Clearing lines shapes the 2D board; entering it in 3D lets you destroy blocks directly with weapons. We haven't opened it — this is off the store page — but the group chat took roughly forty-five seconds to reach 'okay, when.'

Items, weapons and upgrades are designed to create cross-mode synergies, and bosses test whether the player understands both rule sets. That is the entire game, mechanically: a roguelite built around a mechanical punchline that either deepens or doesn't. We're rooting for it to deepen, because the alternative is a very good demo and a short third hour.

Why it escaped the group chat

  • Runs alternate between 2D block-stacking and 3D first-person combat.
  • Weapons, items and upgrades are designed to create cross-mode synergies.
  • Boss encounters test mastery of stacking and shooting together, not separately.

Three dads enter

01

Dad Who Skipped the Tutorial

The concept explains itself before the title does.

I understand blocks. I understand shooting. Combining the two takes me about eight seconds to grasp and slightly longer to laugh at, which makes the first minute completely painless. The roguelite upgrade layer gets more demanding later, so the game has to earn my second hour on more than the opening gag.

02

The Descent Guy

The best runs should start on the stacking screen.

I'll only stay for the version where block placement creates opportunities for the combat phase — where my 2D setup is the reason a 3D room is winnable. If upgrades only raise damage numbers, the two-mode structure loses its reason to exist. This mechanic is either a whole game or a very clever demo.

03

Normal-ish Dad

This is built for one-more-run handoffs.

Short, visible mistakes and an unusual premise make Blocks for Babies easy to pass around, and I've already flagged it as a game-night surprise. Whether it lasts depends on bosses and build variety keeping the joke from wearing thin. If it becomes 'switch screens for damage,' everyone's going to figure that out fast.

Who it is for and what to watch

Invite these people

  • Players who like genre mashups with an immediate arcade loop
  • Roguelite fans who enjoy discovering odd upgrade combinations
  • Game-night spectators who want readable failures and quick resets

Dad caveats

  • Whether the two modes meaningfully affect each other
  • Run variety once the first strong gimmick wears off
  • How much downtime sits between stacking and shooting

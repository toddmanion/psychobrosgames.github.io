The thirty-second version

A pirate map that rewards the long route.

Pirates, dragons, and a map we will absolutely hold upside down.

We haven't played it, but Draco and the Seven Scales makes a strong case for putting the ship back into pirate adventure. Different sea routes open different dungeons, items and puzzles, which gives travel a strategic point instead of turning it into loading screens. If the two halves genuinely feed each other, this could be the throwback pick we all quietly wanted.

Okay, what is this thing?

Draco is a pirate captain trying to save what matters most to him, which is a solid amount of stakes for a pixel-art adventure. The game alternates between top-down exploration on foot and travel by ship across a large world, and the trailer is confident enough about it that we spent a whole coffee break arguing which half we're more excited about. We haven't played it. We're picturing it.

The pitch that got us was route choice. Different passages lead to different dungeons, puzzles and items, so the sea between islands isn't scenery. Rival captains, armadas and sea monsters make the choice pay off or hurt, depending. That's a lot of moving parts, and whether they lock together or drift depends on whether the map keeps meaningful options open past hour six.

Why it escaped the group chat

  • Exploration alternates between on-foot top-down adventuring and sailing between islands.
  • Different sea routes unlock distinct dungeons, items and puzzles.
  • Boss threats include rival captains, armadas, and honest-to-goodness sea monsters.

Three dads enter

01

Dad Who Skipped the Tutorial

Familiar top-down language means I don't need a manual.

I've played enough top-down adventures to recognise a door. Movement, dungeons and bosses are easy to understand, which means the ship layer needs to add possibility without turning basic getting-around into homework. If I can pick a route and just go, I'm going. If I have to consult a chart, I'm not.

02

The Descent Guy

Navigation choices need to actually stick.

I'm watching one thing: whether the route I pick changes what I see later. Multiple sea routes are worth something when they gate resources, equipment and future options in ways that persist. If every path eventually becomes mandatory, the map is just a checklist, and I'll say so at length.

03

Normal-ish Dad

A strong genre shape with one useful new idea.

I like classic top-down adventure and I don't need it reinvented. Pixel-art dungeons are familiar territory, but sailing that actually means something is exactly the sort of small change that keeps this from feeling like a game I already played in 2011. The balance between land and sea is going to decide the whole thing.

Who it is for and what to watch

Invite these people

  • Players who love classic top-down adventure with a modern brain
  • Explorers who want route choice to unlock different content
  • Pirate-game fans who want the ship to matter for once

Dad caveats

  • How much travel time sits between the interesting bits
  • Whether alternate routes stay genuine choices instead of collectibles
  • The balance between exploration, puzzles and boss combat
